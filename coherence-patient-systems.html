Case Study

Coherence in a Sea of
Patient Systems

Client Fortune 5 Health Services Enterprise
Role CX Strategy & Service Design Lead
Focus Service Blueprinting, CX Measurement, Digital Maturity

A national care delivery network was expanding fast. Every acquisition brought another physician group, another regional brand, and another stack of patient-facing tools: portals, call center platforms, scheduling systems, telehealth vendors, marketing platforms, and EHRs with their own logic and data structures.

For patients, this showed up as friction: logins that worked in one portal but not another, call centers that could see part of their history but not the full picture, surveys asking different questions about what "good care" meant.

For leadership, it showed up as something more fundamental: a sea of patient systems that didn't agree on who the patient was, what their journey looked like, or how to measure whether the experience was getting better.

The organization wasn't starting from zero. There was already an enterprise CX measurement framework: a defined way of thinking about satisfaction, loyalty, and experience. But it was implemented unevenly across care delivery organizations, legacy stacks, and newly acquired businesses. The real question wasn't what to measure so much as:

How do we get all of this technology and all of these clinics to measure the same thing in the same way, and tie it back to real journeys?

Our work was about building coherence on top of what existed, not sweeping it away.

Walking into the complexity

There was a clear gap between the ambition of "one experience" and the reality on the ground. Each acquired group brought its own view of how patients should engage: its own portal, telehealth solution, call center setup, and survey approach.

In some regions, enterprise CX measures like NPS were fully adopted and rigorously reported. In others, local surveys and homegrown metrics ruled. Digital teams had rich analytics in their own dashboards. Call centers had detailed operational reports. Clinical teams tracked quality and outcomes in the EHR. But there was no single place where these views came together as the patient experience.

Two executives could look at different dashboards and tell completely different stories about whether experience was improving. New acquisitions added more variation on top.

Leadership wanted a way to look across the care delivery network and answer simple, hard questions:

Are we making it easier for patients to navigate care?

Are we using digital tools in ways that fit how patients actually live their lives?

Are we improving experience in a way that shows up consistently in the existing CX framework?

Making the mess visible

Before any blueprinting, we did the unglamorous work: making the ecosystem visible.

We ran a MarTech and digital capability assessment across regional entities, cataloguing how each one attracted, served, and retained patients. That meant portals and websites, but also call flows, SMS and email journeys, CRM implementations, telehealth tools, and analytics. Each capability was mapped against a shared digital framework so a scheduler in one region could be meaningfully compared to a scheduler in another, even if the vendor names were different.

Then we layered measurement on top. Where did the enterprise CX framework show up? Where did local surveys override it? Which interactions generated consistent metrics, and which were effectively invisible?

Patterns emerged quickly: mature adoption of standard measures in some regions but thin digital analytics; strong digital dashboards in others but weak alignment to the CX scorecard; in a few places, basic metrics like portal logins or first-contact resolution weren't instrumented in a way that could be rolled up centrally.

The result was an honest picture, region by region:

What platforms existed and how they overlapped.

Where obvious consolidation opportunities sat.

How well the CX framework had been operationalized.

The assessment became an x-ray: a way for leaders to see the skeleton of their engagement ecosystem, not just the surface of a single portal or call flow.

Designing a blueprint people could actually use

With the current state visible, the next step was to design a future-state service architecture that felt human enough for non-technical leaders to own and concrete enough for technology and operations teams to implement.

We anchored the work around a single, representative patient: William, a 65-year-old Medicare member managing diabetes. Instead of starting with platforms, we started with his life. How did he first hear about the organization? How did he choose a provider? What happened the first time he tried to schedule an appointment? How did his experience change when he needed telehealth, a prescription renewal, or help understanding his lab results?

William's journey became the spine of the blueprint. Along that spine, we mapped his actions, touchpoints, and emotional state at each step. Underneath, we charted the operations and systems that made those touchpoints possible: call center workflows, CRM records, EHR events, portal experiences, telehealth scheduling, and outbound care management.

At key moments, we attached the CX framework. If William called the contact center, we defined how and when satisfaction would be measured, which operational metrics mattered at that step, and how that data should flow into a central view. If he scheduled online, we identified the behaviors that would tell us whether the experience worked: task completion, abandonment, repeat calls for the same issue.

This wasn't a fictional "perfect state" stack. We annotated the blueprint with real platforms already in use or planned—CRM and orchestration, content and portals, contact center, EHR, analytics. Where a capability didn't exist, we called it out explicitly as a gap.

The blueprinting process itself was as valuable as the artifact. Marketing, IT, clinical operations, and data teams all sat in the same sessions and walked through William's journey step by step. Many saw, for the first time, how their piece of the stack affected what a patient actually felt and where patients were falling through the cracks.

The outcome was a shared map leaders could point to when they said "this is the experience we are trying to deliver," rather than a collection of loosely related projects and dashboards.

Mapping patient journeys across fragmented systems to create a unified experience

Teaching the metrics to see the journey

The organization didn't need another CX framework. It needed the existing one to see more of the real world.

Once the blueprint was in place, we used it to extend and centralize how the CX model was implemented. Metrics stopped being a separate reporting layer and became part of the architecture. At each stage of William's journey, we defined what should be measured, how, and where that data should land.

NPS and CSAT were tied to specific moments, not just channels. First-contact resolution became a signal of whether the system around the agent gave enough context to help. Digital adoption reflected whether online journeys actually worked better than picking up the phone.

We then made it practical. Different regions would implement the same measures in consistent ways: standardized survey triggers, response options, timing, and data definitions so "post-visit NPS" or "portal task completion" meant the same thing everywhere.

On top of that, we defined what a central CX view should look like: a dashboard that could show regional performance side by side, trends over time, and drill-downs to the journeys and clinics driving those numbers.

The message was simple: the CX framework is staying. What's changing is how tightly it's connected to the journeys you deliver and the systems you run.

Early signs of coherence

This kind of transformation plays out over years, not quarters, but early shifts were visible.

The service blueprint became more than a workshop artifact. It started to function as a North Star for engagement decisions. When a region considered a new portal feature, a workflow change, or a local survey, leaders could ask: does this make William's journey more coherent, and will it show up in the enterprise CX view?

The technology roadmap shifted from "what's the next tool?" to "what do we need to standardize so this architecture actually works?" That translated into commitments around a universal patient identity layer, a consolidated enterprise portal, stronger consent and preference management, and a clear set of platforms for new acquisitions to adopt.

In pilot regions, the combination of architectural clarity and measurement alignment started to show in the numbers. One clinic group that migrated to the unified portal and promoted self-service saw online appointment booking jump from roughly a fifth of appointments to about half, with a corresponding reduction in routine scheduling calls. Because the CX framework was wired into the flow, leadership could see not just fewer calls but how satisfaction and repeat engagement moved as a result.

Elsewhere, connecting data across systems allowed analytics teams to spot gaps in follow-up care for diabetic patients and trigger targeted outreach. The interventions themselves weren't new. The difference was that they could now be measured consistently, read through the same CX lens as other parts of the business, and tied back to the service blueprint.

More subtly, internal conversations shifted. Arguments that used to hinge on whose dashboard was "right" moved toward which parts of the journey needed work, which systems were helping or hindering, and how changes would show up in the CX framework everyone recognized.
