Case Study

Coherence in a Sea of
Patient Systems

Client Fortune 5 Health Services Enterprise
Role CX Strategy & Service Design Lead
Focus Service Blueprinting

Our client, a leading national healthcare organization, embarked on an aggressive expansion strategy across North America, primarily through acquisitions. Each deal brought in its own stack of portals, call center tools, EHRs, analytics platforms, and local CX practices, a sea of patient systems that did not naturally align with the organization's broader vision. An enterprise CX measurement framework already existed, but it was applied inconsistently across legacy entities and newly acquired groups.

Our role was to help organize and onboard newly acquired medical groups in a way that felt structured and supportive in the first year, while designing a repeatable integration strategy. The integration strategy was deliberately human-centered. Rather than starting with consolidation targets and platform decisions, we began with how patients experienced care across this fragmented landscape and used that as the anchor for technology, data, and operational choices.

That meant aligning technologies, data models, operational workflows, and CX measurement into a service architecture the client could reuse as the acquisition pipeline continued to grow, without losing sight of the people at the center of the system.

Initial Assessment and Understanding

We began with a deep assessment of the operational and technological capabilities of the newly acquired organizations. For each medical group, we built an "x-ray" of how they actually worked: technology stacks, data models, integrations, workflows, contact center operations, digital front doors, and the distinctive aspects of their member and patient experience.

Equally important, we mapped how the existing enterprise CX framework showed up in practice. Some groups were fully aligned to standard measures; others ran their own surveys and tracked success in local dashboards. We documented where experience metrics were captured, where they were missing, and where different systems were effectively measuring the same thing in different ways.

As the work progressed, it became clear that acquisition-led growth was not a temporary spike, it was the operating model. That shifted our focus from "how do we integrate these specific groups?" to "how do we help the client adopt a broader, reusable integration and operating strategy across all current and future medical systems, without losing the human context that makes those systems meaningful?"

Service Design Architecture and Brand Alignment

With the landscape visible, we worked with leaders to identify the core elements of the brand experience and the outcomes that needed to be measured consistently, regardless of local systems. Instead of treating technology, operations, and CX as separate tracks, we developed a service design architecture that ran from front-stage patient experience down through operations, platforms, data, and measurement.

Crucially, the architecture was built from the patient outward. We framed it as a service blueprint that tells the story of how a real person moves through the system, what they are trying to accomplish at each step, and how internal teams and technology need to coordinate around them. That blueprint became the central artifact that different functions could gather around, rather than each defending their own roadmap or platform.

Chronic care was selected as the primary journey for the initial work. It cuts across specialties, depends on robust handovers, and represents a long-term relationship between patient and system. It also surfaced many of the organization's hardest problems: fragmented data, inconsistent follow-up, and variation in how "success" was defined.

The resulting service architecture described what a coherent chronic care experience should look like across the network. It showed how portals, call centers, EHRs, CRM, and analytics work together; where responsibilities sit; and how the existing CX framework attaches to moments that actually matter to patients, not just to systems.

Patient Interviews and Persona Development

To ground the work in reality, we conducted in-person interviews with patients managing chronic conditions, using diabetes as the primary use case for accessibility and reach. We spoke with patients from across the ecosystem, individuals served by newly acquired groups and those already within the larger system, to capture different histories, expectations, and levels of trust.

From this research, we developed robust personas that went beyond demographics. They reflected daily routines, digital comfort, barriers to care, and the social and financial factors that shaped whether treatment plans were realistic. These personas became the lens through which we evaluated every step in the journey and every proposed change to platforms and workflows.

Internally, this sent a clear signal: patients, not platforms, were the organizing principle. The human stories collected in interviews were brought into strategy sessions so that technology and operations decisions were continuously tested against a simple question: would this actually help someone like this manage their care with more confidence and less effort?

Journey Mapping and Opportunities for Improvement

Mapping the chronic care journey end to end revealed a series of predictable breakpoints: handovers between operating units, missing or delayed data, unclear ownership of follow-up, and blind spots in measurement. We treated each step as both a human scenario and a system pattern.

For example, when a patient moved from an in-clinic visit to remote monitoring and follow-up, we highlighted where identity broke, where context was lost between systems, and where no one was accountable for closing the loop. From there, we translated these scenarios into concrete service requirements: which teams needed what data, which platforms needed to talk to each other, and how success at that step would be measured in the existing CX framework.

The service blueprint was not produced in isolation. We used it actively in cross-functional workshops with marketing, IT, clinical leaders, and operations teams. Seeing their work visualized along the same patient storyline helped teams understand their interdependencies and sparked more collaborative, patient-centered conversations. Instead of arguing about individual tickets or features, teams could see where and how their decisions made the journey clearer or more confusing for real people.

The outcome was a chronic care service design architecture that clearly spelled out the operational, technological, and data requirements needed to deliver a consistent experience over time, and a set of prioritized opportunities where targeted changes would make the biggest difference for patients and staff.

Unified Measurement and Social Determinants of Health

Rather than invent a new scorecard, we extended and operationalized the client's existing CX measurement framework so it could see more of the real journey. We defined how core metrics, including satisfaction, loyalty, digital adoption, and continuity of care, should attach to key moments in the chronic care experience and how they should be implemented consistently across operating units.

Growth and stability in a patient's health became the most important patient-facing indicator that operations and infrastructure were working. To interpret these outcomes correctly, we incorporated social determinants of health into the model, including income stability, housing, access to transportation, caregiving responsibilities, and digital access. This allowed the organization to see where similar clinical pathways produced different outcomes for different populations and where changes to outreach, support services, or channel strategy would matter most.

The result was a unified measurement structure that could be shared across all operating units, aligning enterprise CX measures, clinical outcomes, and contextual factors around a single view of value created for patients, not just for systems.

Scaling Best Practices Across the Ecosystem

As we assessed each acquired organization, we found pockets of excellence: a newly acquired group with highly effective care coordinators, a regional contact center with strong first-contact resolution on chronic care questions, a local clinic with a well-designed digital follow-up program.

Instead of forcing everyone to conform to the lowest common denominator, we codified these strengths into patterns and wrapped them into the service architecture. High-performing processes, workflows, and digital practices were documented in a way that could be replicated elsewhere, not as rigid scripts but as proven models tied back to the patient journey.

The service blueprint became the vehicle for this scaling. It gave teams a shared visual reference for how best practices from one region could be adapted elsewhere without losing their human intent. Teams in different markets could see where a practice fit in the journey, who needed to be involved, and which metrics would show whether it was working.

This gave the client something they had not had before: a scalable integration and improvement playbook. New acquisitions could be onboarded into a coherent service architecture, adopt a unified measurement structure, and benefit from human-centered, cross-functional practices discovered elsewhere in the network. Over time, this shifted the organization from "absorbing" acquired groups one by one to deliberately shaping a more consistent, collaborative, and patient-centered ecosystem.
