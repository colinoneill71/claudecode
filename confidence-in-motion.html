Case Study

Confidence in Motion:
Connecting people, processes and platforms

Client Global Auto Rental Company
Role CX Strategy & Service Design
Focus Operations and Service Architecture

A charcoal gray Jeep Grand Cherokee.

Leather seats. Wood trim. The fancy CarPlay thing.

It took a while, but Maria had found the perfect rental car for her trip. Big enough for the group, a reasonable price and what looked like an easy pick up at the airport (no shuttle bus).

Then she called to book it. The agent, Hector was friendly, but he couldn't guarantee that she'd get the car that she saw on the screen in front of her. He also couldn't commit to a final price or the car she'd booked. Frustrated Hector suggested that she cancel the online booking and directly contact the rental center to check on their expected Jeep inventory.

Almost half of our clients' bookings were happening by phone. Even with a well-designed site and app, many customers had a different mental model of their rental and wanted to get to a human confirmation of what they saw in a screen. The agents at the call center and on-line were navigating a collision of independent systems for reservations, inventory, billing, loyalty, etc., each operating with its own view of the world and unaware of the others. It was up to Maria and Hector to put the puzzle pieces of this fragmented infrastructure together to try to create an integrated service.

Fragmented Infrastructure

The consequences were significant: over 20% of reservation calls failed to convert, and 15% of calls were purely for rate shopping. Millions of dollars in bookings were lost annually because the service architecture did not match customers' expectations or brand promises. Meanwhile, the industry was evolving: peer-to-peer rentals, rideshares, waymos, EVs with sensitive sensors, autonomous driving features and incompatible networks. Vehicle telemetry has shifted. The intricate model of a discrete rental transaction was being eroded by a more fluid, dynamic and distributed model that promised more choice, and delivered more complexity.

In conversations in executive offices, off-shore customer service centers and airport rental centers, the same pattern emerged: agents and travelers were expected to navigate a collision of fragmented systems. The agents knew it. The customers felt it. The business measured it—but the underlying issue wasn't technology. It was the service architecture that connected (or failed to connect) all of it.
